FAIR QuickRisk

Methodology, limitations and disclaimer

Estimate your likely annual cyber-loss across five common scenarios using the active FAIR-QK-2.0 posterior-predictive model — an indication, not a full FAIR analysis, an audit, a certification or professional advice.

Start the assessment

What the application does

FAIR QuickRisk asks eight organisation-profile questions and exactly two questions for each of five predefined scenarios. A Bayesian reference-class posterior, informed by documented public evidence and your answers, supplies correlated frequency, materiality and severity parameters. The server then simulates 5,000 years per scenario: each year draws a Poisson count of material-loss events and a severity for every event, and the scenario and portfolio annual totals are summarised.

The active model is FAIR-QK-2.0 · schema 2.0.0 · calibration CAL-2.0. It reports expected annual loss, posterior-predictive P10/P50/P90 annual totals, the chance of exceeding key thresholds, and an estimate-precision indicator for each scenario.

Why these five scenarios

The five scenarios are a practical rapid-assessment set chosen for current threat evidence and broad applicability, not a mathematically exact ranking of the five most frequent cyber risks. Public threat reporting identifies phishing and social engineering as a leading initial access route, vulnerability exploitation as a major and growing vector, ransomware as the most impactful incident type, third-party involvement as a large and increasing share of breaches, and denial-of-service as the highest-volume disruption activity.

Incident frequency is not treated as business risk. A very frequent scenario such as denial-of-service is not automatically ranked highly, because the model also considers financial loss magnitude.

What is borrowed from FAIR, and what is simplified

The model borrows FAIR's core idea: separate "how often" from "how costly", use ranges rather than single numbers, and keep a primary loss apart from a possible knock-on (secondary) loss. The technical names — threat event frequency, loss event frequency, loss magnitude, primary loss and secondary loss — are each explained in plain language inside the application.

  • A full FAIR analysis decomposes frequency and magnitude into many factors. This application uses a small fixed factor set and posterior reference classes.
  • Only five scenarios are assessed; other material scenarios are outside the model.
  • Scenario dependence is represented by shared posterior draws and a shared organisation attack rate. This is a documented structural dependence model, not an empirically estimated correlation matrix.
  • Each material event draws one total positive severity, split between primary and secondary loss by an uncertain allocation. The split is an elicitation assumption rather than two separately estimated severity models.
  • Statutory fines, regulatory penalties and specific legal liabilities are explicitly out of scope.
  • Annual material-event counts are Poisson draws and per-event severities are summed. Zero-event years are zero-loss years, so the reported percentiles describe simulated annual totals.
  • The model does not capture all uncertainty about its own structure, distribution shapes, calibration, transportability or organisation-specific dependence.
  • National reference evidence is not organisation-specific prospective validation. Scenario weights and questionnaire multipliers remain explicit elicitation assumptions.

How answers become posterior evidence

The reference posterior supplies organisation-size attack prevalence, material-cost probability and positive-severity distributions. A likelihood answer applies an uncertain frequency multiplier to that scenario's share; an impact answer applies an uncertain severity multiplier. Organisation-profile answers act as uncertain modifiers: exposure and maturity affect frequency or materiality, while disruption cost, sensitive-information exposure and recovery capability affect severity.

The likelihood question asks how often the scenario is attempted or contacted in a typical 12-month period, before considering whether your defences would stop it. Controls therefore enter through the maturity modifier without being counted twice. An Unknown answer retains the reference posterior rather than inventing a favourable or adverse extreme, and it reduces the estimate-precision rating. An optional material-loss event count can be supplied; it updates the shared posterior through a Poisson likelihood and is held in memory only.

What "typical year" and "bad year (1 in 10)" mean

"Typical year" is the middle of the simulated annual totals: half of the simulated years are cheaper, half costlier. "Bad year (1 in 10)" is the level that only 1 in 10 simulated years is worse than. "Best case (1 in 10)" is the level that only 1 in 10 simulated years falls below. The technical names for these are P50 (the median), P90 (the 90th percentile) and P10 (the 10th percentile).

These are posterior-predictive intervals under the documented model. Because material-event counts are often zero and severity is heavy-tailed, P50 can be zero and the mean can exceed P90. A wide typical-to-bad range is a statement about uncertainty in the inputs, evidence and assumptions — not a confidence interval around a known true value and not a forecast for a particular organisation.

What expected annual loss means

Expected annual loss is the arithmetic mean of the simulated annual total losses for a scenario, including zero-loss years. It is a long-run model mean, not the most likely outcome and not a forecast for the coming year. The portfolio mean is the sum of scenario means, while portfolio percentiles are calculated from the year-by-year joint portfolio totals. That joint result depends on the documented shared-posterior dependence model and is not organisation-specific validation.

Versioning and evidence

The model identifier, model version, schema version, calibration version, methodology version and evidence package version are shown on every result and report. Scenario definitions, questions, answer options and the read-only posterior package live outside the public web root and are version controlled. Activation requires a hash-verified, non-synthetic package whose calibration report is validated. Historical 1.x calculations retain their original model and methodology identifiers.

Your data and retention

The QuickRisk questionnaire is zero-retention: your answers, the optional material-loss event count and calculated results are processed transiently to produce this assessment, and the application does not store them in a database, account, browser storage, cookie or application file. The optional event count is held only in page memory, is excluded from saved reports, and no account or login is required. If you close or refresh the page, the assessment cannot be recovered by the application.

The optional QuickRisk Live overlay is separate. If you use it and explicitly save an assessment, the application stores that saved assessment — and the anonymous session it belongs to — in its own database so you can reopen, archive or share it. No name, email or organisation is attached, share links are read-only and revocable, and the only cookie Live or Decision Lab uses is the opaque session cookie that JavaScript cannot read.

The optional Decision Lab is separate too. If you explicitly save a treatment analysis, it stores the computed comparison, the portfolio search, the treatment selection and the anonymous session in the same database so you can reopen, archive or share the Board Risk Pack. It does not store the questionnaire answers and cannot recompute them from the saved record.

Normal web infrastructure may process technical metadata such as source IP address, timestamp, HTTP status and user-agent. Assessment answers and results are never attached to those records. The application can prohibit intentional application-level persistence, but it cannot control transient copies that may exist outside its architecture, such as browser memory, operating-system memory management, network infrastructure or security monitoring controlled by the hosting provider.

Evidence sources and their roles

Calibration is evidence-informed, not a claim of organisation-specific validation. The active package records each source's permitted and prohibited uses:

  • DSIT Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/2026 — organisation-size attack prevalence, material-cost probability and positive-cost severity quantiles.
  • IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 — large-enterprise severity sensitivity only; its mean is not pooled with DSIT respondent quantiles.
  • Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report 2026 — conditional scenario-composition context and sensitivity only; it is not an organisation-level annual frequency source.
  • European Central Bank euro reference rates — 2025 currency-conversion provenance.
  • FAIR Institute — FAIR Standard (conceptual reference only).

Questionnaire multipliers, scenario weights, the primary/secondary allocation and the IBM distribution shape are labelled as assumptions in the calibration package. The known validation gap — no independent organisation-level prospective loss dataset — is disclosed in every result response.
