FAIR QuickRisk

Methodology, limitations and disclaimer

Estimate your likely annual cyber-loss across five common scenarios — an indication, not a full FAIR analysis, an audit, a certification or professional advice.

Start the assessment

What the application does

FAIR QuickRisk asks eight organisation-profile questions and exactly two questions for each of five predefined scenarios, then runs a server-side Monte Carlo simulation of 5,000 simulated years per scenario. It reports expected annual loss, a typical-year to bad-year (1 in 10) range, the chance of exceeding key thresholds, and an estimate-precision indicator for each scenario.

Why these five scenarios

The five scenarios are a practical rapid-assessment set chosen for current threat evidence and broad applicability, not a mathematically exact ranking of the five most frequent cyber risks. Public threat reporting identifies phishing and social engineering as a leading initial access route, vulnerability exploitation as a major and growing vector, ransomware as the most impactful incident type, third-party involvement as a large and increasing share of breaches, and denial-of-service as the highest-volume disruption activity.

Incident frequency is not treated as business risk. A very frequent scenario such as denial-of-service is not automatically ranked highly, because the model also considers financial loss magnitude.

What is borrowed from FAIR, and what is simplified

The model borrows FAIR's core idea: separate "how often" from "how costly", use ranges rather than single numbers, and keep a primary loss apart from a possible knock-on (secondary) loss. The technical names — threat event frequency, loss event frequency, loss magnitude, primary loss and secondary loss — are each explained in plain language inside the application.

  • A full FAIR analysis decomposes frequency and magnitude into many factors. This application uses a small fixed factor set.
  • Only five scenarios are assessed; other material scenarios are outside the model.
  • Scenario dependencies and correlations are not modelled, so no joint portfolio percentile is presented.
  • Secondary loss is represented by one occurrence probability and one magnitude distribution per scenario.
  • Statutory fines, regulatory penalties and specific legal liabilities are explicitly out of scope.
  • One loss magnitude is sampled per simulated trial rather than once per event, so the reported values are an annualised exposure approximation and not realised annual loss totals.
  • The model does not capture uncertainty about its own structure, distribution shapes, calibration or scenario correlation.

How answers become ranges

Each answer maps to a range stored in versioned model files rather than to a single number. The likelihood question asks how often the scenario is attempted or contacted in a typical 12-month period, before considering whether your defences would stop it: that answer is "how often" (technical name: threat event frequency), and your defences are applied separately and only once, so the same control is never counted twice. An impact answer maps to a range of multipliers against your organisation's typical daily disruption cost. Organisation-profile answers act as modifiers. An Unknown answer is modelled as an equal-weight mixture of the available answers, which widens the range, keeps the central estimate inside the range of the answers you could have chosen, and reduces the estimate-precision rating.

What "typical year" and "bad year (1 in 10)" mean

"Typical year" is the middle of the simulated outcomes: half of the simulated years are cheaper, half costlier. "Bad year (1 in 10)" is the level that only 1 in 10 simulated years is worse than. "Best case (1 in 10)" is the level that only 1 in 10 simulated years falls below. The technical names for these are P50 (the median), P90 (the 90th percentile) and P10 (the 10th percentile). They describe an annualised exposure approximation, not realised annual loss totals. A wide typical-to-bad range is a statement about uncertainty in your inputs and assumptions — not a confidence interval around a known true value.

What expected annual loss means

Expected annual loss is the arithmetic mean of the simulated annualised exposure values for a scenario: the long-run average of the modelled approximation, not the most likely outcome and not a forecast for the coming year. The total shown is the sum of the five scenario expected annual losses, which is exact for expected values. Because dependencies between scenarios are not modelled, that sum is not presented as a joint distribution percentile.

Versioning

The model identifier, model version, schema version and calibration version are shown on every result and report. Scenario definitions, questions, answer options and calibration parameters live in version-controlled model files outside the public web root. Calibration changes create a new version. Because the questionnaire retains no assessment, a calibration change cannot alter any past assessment.

Your data and retention

The QuickRisk questionnaire is zero-retention: your answers and calculated results are processed transiently to produce this assessment, and the application does not store them in a database, account, browser storage, cookie or application file. No account or login is required. If you close or refresh the page, the assessment cannot be recovered by the application.

The optional QuickRisk Live overlay is separate. If you use it and explicitly save an assessment, the application stores that saved assessment — and the anonymous session it belongs to — in its own database so you can reopen, archive or share it. No name, email or organisation is attached, share links are read-only and revocable, and the only cookie Live uses is the opaque session cookie that JavaScript cannot read.

Normal web infrastructure may process technical metadata such as source IP address, timestamp, HTTP status and user-agent. Assessment answers and results are never attached to those records. The application can prohibit intentional application-level persistence, but it cannot control transient copies that may exist outside its architecture, such as browser memory, operating-system memory management, network infrastructure or security monitoring controlled by the hosting provider.

Contextual evidence sources

The scenario set is informed by current public threat reporting. Those sources provide contextual justification only and are not used as direct model coefficients.
